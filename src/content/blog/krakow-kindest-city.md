Every hour, on the hour, a trumpeter appears in the tallest window of St. Mary's Basilica and plays the *hejnał* — a five-note call that breaks off mid-phrase, honouring (the story goes) a watchman shot mid-warning by a Tatar archer in 1241. He plays it in four directions, day and night, as trumpeters have for centuries. On my third evening, sitting in the great square with a coffee as the light went amber, I realised I had stopped planning and started living there. Kraków does that quickly.

## The case for Kraków

I keep a private shortlist of European cities for solo travelers who want maximum reward for minimum logistics. Kraków sits near the top, for concrete reasons:

- **It's flat.** The entire medieval centre and the Kazimierz district can be walked without one meaningful hill. The single climb — Wawel Hill, to the castle and cathedral — is a five-minute ramp.
- **It's compact and legible.** The Old Town sits inside the Planty, a four-kilometre ring of park where the city walls once stood. You physically cannot get lost: walk in any direction and you hit trees, reset, try again. The Planty benches, in chestnut shade, may be Europe's best urban resting infrastructure.
- **It's gentle on the budget.** Poland remains startling value. A serious lunch at a *bar mleczny* — the subsidised "milk bars" that survived communism because everyone loved them too much to let them close — runs a few euros. My spotless hotel inside the Planty cost what a mediocre airport lodge costs in England.
- **Coffee-house culture welcomes the unaccompanied.** Kraków's café tradition is Viennese in origin: a table, a newspaper, an unhurried hour. No one has ever been rushed out of a Kraków café, and a party of one with a book is the house's most traditional customer.

## Seven centuries in a square kilometre

The Rynek Główny is Europe's largest medieval square, and the architecture lesson radiates outward from it: the Gothic asymmetry of St. Mary's two mismatched towers, the Renaissance arcades of the Cloth Hall, baroque church fronts, Art Nouveau café interiors, and the sober courtyards of the Jagiellonian University, teaching since 1364.

Then cross to **Kazimierz**, the old Jewish quarter — seven synagogues, from the 15th-century Old Synagogue to the ornate Tempel, standing within a few hundred metres of each other. Its history is heavy; its present is full of life — klezmer music drifting out of Szeroka Street in the evenings, the best breakfasts in the city, and bookshops that expect you to stay a while.

> Kraków asks almost nothing of your knees, your wallet, or your nerves — and pays out in centuries.

## Day trips that matter

Two excursions, both easy without a car: the **Wieliczka Salt Mine**, a UNESCO underworld of chapels carved in salt (be aware it's some 800 steps down, taken gradually; there's a lift back up), and **Auschwitz-Birkenau**, ninety minutes away. I went alone, which I quietly recommend — it is an experience you'll want to process at your own pace, without conversation. Book the guided study visit well ahead; go, bear witness, and take the evening afterwards for yourself in the Planty.

## Practical notes

Fly into Kraków's airport and take the train — eighteen minutes to the central station, which sits an eight-minute level walk from the square. June and September are ideal; December's Christmas market on the Rynek is worth braving the cold for, with mulled wine measured generously.

Learn three words — *dzień dobry*, *proszę*, *dziękuję* (good day, please, thank you) — and deploy them shamelessly. Poles of my generation and older often speak German or Russian rather than English; the younger staff speak everything. Between the two, a smiling traveler manages perfectly.
