I came to drones late — my first flight was at 63, in a Northumberland field, with the manual open on my phone and my heart rate somewhere in the anaerobic zone. Four years on, aerial footage is the reason my grandchildren watch my travel films to the end. Nothing communicates *why you went* like the moment the camera lifts over a ridge line.

The Dolomites in late September — larches turning, first snow dusting the towers of the Tre Cime — are the finest aerial subject in Europe. They are also a perfect case study in the one skill that matters more than piloting: knowing where you're allowed to fly.

## The EU rules, in plain English

The good news: Europe harmonised its drone rules, so one framework covers the whole EU. If your drone weighs **under 250 grams** (the sub-250 class most travel drones now target), life is comparatively simple:

- **Register once, fly anywhere in the EU.** You register as an operator in one member state, put that ID number on the aircraft, and it's valid across all of them.
- You fly in the "Open" category, subclass A1 — which means you can fly near (but never over) uninvolved people, no exam required for the lightest class, though the online training is short and genuinely worth doing.
- **Keep it in sight, under 120 metres, and away from airports.** Those three habits cover most of the law and nearly all of the safety.

The catch — and it's the one that bites visitors — is that **national parks and nature reserves make their own rules**, and in Italy most of them, including the Tre Cime nature park and much of the Dolomites' protected core, prohibit recreational drones outright. The rules exist to protect nesting golden eagles and the silence everyone climbed up there to find. Respect them without grumbling; there is always legal sky nearby.

## Where I actually flew

Outside the protected zones there is spectacular, legal flying. My rule is to check three sources the night before — the official *d-flight* map for Italy, the park authority's own site, and the drone app's built-in geofencing — and to fly at dawn, when thermals are calm, light is best, and trailheads are empty.

The footage I'm proudest of came from the Passo Giau road corridors and meadows below Cortina — takeoff from a pull-out, ninety seconds up over the hairpins as the sun hit Ra Gusela, ninety seconds back. Four minutes of battery, a clip I've watched a hundred times.

> The drone doesn't replace being there. It explains being there — to the people at home who wonder why you keep going back alone.

## Why small drones suit us

A sub-250g drone weighs less than my reading glasses case and folds smaller than a sandwich. That's not just a legal category; it's an ergonomic philosophy for the older traveler:

- No tripod-lugging: it *is* the tripod, the crane, and the dolly.
- The modern ones hover rock-steady and fly themselves home when the battery runs low or your attention does.
- Pre-programmed orbit and reveal moves produce smooth "professional" shots with zero joystick finesse — the aircraft does the choreography, you do the directing.

My kit for a three-week trip: the drone, three batteries, one spare set of propellers, all in a case the size of a paperback. It shares the bag with a pocket gimbal camera for ground-level walking shots. Total camera department: 900 grams.

## Practical notes

Insurance: some EU countries require third-party liability cover — an annual policy costs less than a nice dinner and settles the question everywhere. Carry batteries in your cabin bag, never checked luggage. And learn the local words for "drone" and "is it permitted?" — asking first has earned me flying tips from Italian rangers, and once, an invitation to film a village festival from above. People forgive a great deal of hobbyist enthusiasm in someone who asks politely first.
