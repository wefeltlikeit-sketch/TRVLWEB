Everyone warns you about Lisbon's hills. Seven of them, cobbled, some at gradients that would qualify as ski slopes elsewhere. What nobody tells you is that Lisbon spent a century and a half building machines so its own citizens wouldn't have to climb — and those machines are the best travel infrastructure in Europe for anyone whose knees have opinions.

## The golden rule: ride up, walk down

My entire Lisbon strategy fits in six words. The city's trams, funiculars, and public lifts haul you to the top of each hill; gravity and the views bring you home.

- The **Elevador da Glória** funicular climbs from Restauradores to the São Pedro de Alcântara viewpoint in ninety seconds. Arrive at 8 a.m. and you'll share the miradouro with two locals and a cat.
- The **Elevador de Santa Justa** — a cathedral-sized wrought-iron lift built by a student of Eiffel's school — connects downtown to the Carmo ruins. Skip the queue by walking in from the top via Largo do Carmo and riding it *down*.
- **Tram 28E** is the famous one, and by mid-morning it is a sardine tin with pickpockets. Ride it at dawn from Martim Moniz, or take **Tram 12E** around Alfama instead — same rattling 1930s carriages, a fraction of the crowd.

## Alfama rewards the slow

Alfama, the Moorish quarter that survived the 1755 earthquake, is not a district you see. It is a district you sit in. The alleys are too narrow for buses and too tangled for tour groups to keep formation, which means the pace inside is set by residents — most of them my age or better — carrying bread, hanging laundry, arguing gently from balconies.

Take the 12E to Largo das Portas do Sol, admire the view over the rooftops to the river, then simply descend. Every wrong turn in Alfama is the right turn. When you need a rest there is always a step, a bench, a tiny café with two tables where a *bica* costs under a euro and no one has ever been hurried.

> A city built with lifts and viewpoints every three hundred metres is a city that was designed, accidentally, for the older traveler. Lisbon doesn't require stamina. It requires a strategy.

## Fado alone is better

I was apprehensive about the classic solo-traveler evening problem: the fado house, built for couples and groups. The answer is the **Fado ao Centro** model — early-evening cultural sessions around 6 p.m., fifty minutes of serious singing with a spoken introduction, no dinner obligation, no minimum spend. You're back on the street by 7:30 with the whole evening ahead and the music still ringing.

For dinner alone, do as the Lisboetas do and eat at the counter. A *tasca* lunch counter treats a party of one as the natural order of things: grilled fish, boiled potatoes, a glass of vinho verde, change from fifteen euros.

## Practical notes

Stay on the flat. The Baixa grid or Chiado's lower edge puts every tram and lift within reach, so the hills become optional attractions rather than daily obstacles. Cobbles are genuinely slippery when wet — Lisbon's beautiful *calçada* pavement has the coefficient of friction of polished marble — so pack shoes with real tread and keep one hand free.

Buy the Viva Viagem card and load it with 24-hour passes; every funicular and lift above is included, which turns 'tourist attractions' costing €4 a ride into ordinary transit.

March and October are the sweet spots: 18 degrees, low season prices, and that famous Atlantic light slanting down the alleys from a sun that never gets high enough to be cruel.
