"You can't do the Cotswolds without a car." I heard it so many times I decided to test it, partly out of stubbornness — a quality that improves with age, like the local cheese.

Verdict: not only can you do the Cotswolds without a car, you can do them *better*. The whole point of these limestone villages is the walking between them — footpaths older than the buildings, worn into the hills by a thousand years of wool merchants, connecting church spire to church spire across sheep-cropped fields. Drive between the honeypot villages and you queue for parking with everyone else. Walk in, and you arrive the way the villages were designed to be arrived at: on foot, over a stile, church tower first.

## The system

Base yourself in **Moreton-in-Marsh** — it has a proper railway station, ninety minutes direct from London Paddington, plus banks, chemists, and a Tuesday market that's run since 1227. From there, two tools:

- **The bus.** The 801 and 802 routes link Moreton to Stow-on-the-Wold, Bourton-on-the-Water, and Broadway reliably enough to plan around. If you hold an English concessionary bus pass, it's free; visitors pay a few pounds. Check the last return time, write it on your hand, and the network is yours.
- **Your feet.** The trick is to ride out and walk back (or the reverse, letting the bus rescue you). Bus to Stow, then the gentle five miles back through the Swells along the River Dikler. Bus to Broadway, walk up to Broadway Tower for the view over twelve counties, wander down through Snowshill — lavender country — at whatever pace the day suggests.

## Distances that respect you

Cotswold walking is the kindest in Britain. Field paths, wool-church waypoints, a pub at almost every hinge point, and nothing you'd call a climb — the highest ground in the whole range is barely 300 metres. My rule was six to eight miles a day with a pub lunch in the middle, which sounds modest and adds up to forty-plus miles in a week of the best scenery in England.

Two favourites nobody drives to, because there's nowhere to park:

- **The Slaughters** at 8 a.m., before the coaches reach Bourton next door. Lower Slaughter's mill stream, one heron, no people.
- **The path from Chipping Campden up Dover's Hill**, where the escarpment drops away and the Vale of Evesham opens up like a map being unrolled.

> A car in the Cotswolds is a machine for skipping the Cotswolds. The stuff between the villages *is* the place.

## The solo dimension

England's pub culture is the great equaliser for solo travelers. A party of one at the bar of The Bell in Moreton or The Queen's Head in Stow is not an anomaly to be managed but a tradition to be honoured. Order the pie, take the corner seat, and within twenty minutes someone's dog will have introduced you to its owner.

B&Bs, too, suit us better than hotels here: single-occupancy rates are negotiable outside summer, and Cotswold landladies remain the world's most reliable source of walking-route intelligence, dispensed at breakfast with the porridge.

## Practical notes

Go in May for the hawthorn and cow parsley, or late September when the beech woods turn and the light goes golden to match the stone. Waterproof boots are non-negotiable in any month; this is still England. Ordnance Survey map OL45 covers nearly everything, or use the OS app — the paths are so well-waymarked that navigation is mostly reassurance.

And book Sunday lunch ahead. Some traditions have queues for good reason.
