Somewhere above the Morteratsch glacier, as the little red train hauled itself around a curve so tight I could see both ends of it at once, I realised I had been smiling for two hours without noticing.

The Bernina line runs four hours from Chur in Switzerland to Tirano in Italy, climbing over 2,200 metres without a single cogwheel — just patience and 55 tunnels and 196 bridges of astonishing Swiss stubbornness. It is a UNESCO World Heritage route, and it is the journey I recommend first to anyone my age who is nervous about traveling alone.

## Why trains are the older solo traveler's best friend

No luggage wrestling at airports. No driving on the wrong side while navigating. Seats face the scenery, there's a toilet aboard, and if you like your fellow passengers you can talk to them — and if you don't, a window seat on the Bernina is the best company in Europe.

More practically: **rail travel is where senior discounts actually live.**

- Switzerland's half-fare travelcard cuts most fares by 50% and pays for itself in days.
- Austria's ÖBB Vorteilscard Senior and France's Carte Avantage Senior do similar work.
- The Eurail Senior Pass takes 10% off for travelers 60 and over, on top of first-class comfort that costs less than people assume.

I travelled first class on the regular regional trains rather than the famous panoramic Bernina Express. Heresy? Hardly. The regional trains cover the identical track, cost less, stop more often — and crucially, **the windows open**. Every photograph in this entry was taken through fresh alpine air rather than curved panoramic glass.

## Breaking the journey

The solo traveler's advantage is that nobody is waiting. I split the route over three days:

- **Chur** — Switzerland's oldest town, with a compact car-free old centre that takes an evening and rewards it.
- **Pontresina** — quieter and cheaper than St. Moritz next door, with a hotel breakfast view I still think about. The flat glacier-valley walk to Morteratsch is the gentlest 'alpine hike' imaginable: a wide gravel path, benches at intervals, and a glacier at the end of it.
- **Poschiavo** — the surprise of the trip. An Italian-speaking Swiss valley town with palazzi built by emigrant pastry chefs who came home rich. Sit in the square, order the local buckwheat *pizzoccheri*, and watch the town go by.

Then the finale: the open-air spiral viaduct at Brusio, the train corkscrewing down into vineyards, and suddenly — palm trees, Italian voices, espresso at Tirano station for a euro and a half.

> The train does the work. You do the looking. After forty years of being the one who drove, that division of labour feels like luxury beyond anything a five-star lobby offers.

## Practical notes

Book seats ahead only in high summer; shoulder season, I simply boarded. Keep your bag small enough to lift overhead yourself — my whole kit for twelve days was one 40-litre pack. Swiss stations have lifts on every platform; Tirano is step-free onto the Italian network if you carry on toward Milan or Lake Como, which you should.

Go in late September. The larches turn gold, the crowds are gone, the snow is fresh on the peaks but not on the platforms. And take the window seat on the right-hand side going south. You can thank me from Poschiavo.
