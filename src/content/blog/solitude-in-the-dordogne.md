There is a particular kind of quiet in the Dordogne valley at seven in the morning. The mist sits low on the river, the honey-coloured stone of the villages has not yet warmed, and for an hour or two the entire medieval world belongs to you.

I spent nine days driving this valley alone, and I want to make the case that it may be the single best self-drive trip in Europe for a solo traveler past sixty. Not because it is easy — though it is — but because it rewards exactly the things we are good at: patience, curiosity, and the willingness to sit still and look at something properly.

## Why the Dordogne works so well alone

The distances are short. From my base in Sarlat-la-Canéda, nothing I wanted to see was more than forty minutes away. That means no long hauls, no fatigue-driven mistakes on unfamiliar roads, and the freedom to go home for a rest after lunch — a freedom I used shamelessly.

The roads themselves are gentle. Well-signed, lightly trafficked outside July and August, with pull-offs conveniently placed at every viewpoint as if the *département* knew a photographer was coming. If you have not driven abroad in a while, this is the place to get your confidence back. Book an automatic well in advance; they are scarce at the smaller rental depots.

## The architecture is the itinerary

I organised each day around a single building or village and refused to rush past it:

- **Sarlat-la-Canéda** — the best-preserved medieval town centre in France. Go at dawn before the market crowds; the lantern of the ancient church of Sainte-Marie, now an indoor market, is worth thirty minutes on its own.
- **La Roque-Gageac** — houses grown straight out of a golden cliff face above the river. The troglodyte fort halfway up the rock is a steep climb; the view from the riverbank is, frankly, just as good, and I say that as someone who did the climb.
- **Beynac-et-Cazenac** — a fortress that Richard the Lionheart besieged, above a village where every doorway deserves a photograph.
- **Domme** — a *bastide* town on a clifftop, laid out on a grid in 1281. Walk the ramparts in the late afternoon when the coach parties have gone.

> Traveling alone means never having to apologise for spending forty-five minutes looking at a single carved doorway. That is not a small thing. That is the whole point.

## The gabare and the drone

Twice I let someone else do the driving. The flat-bottomed *gabare* boats out of La Roque-Gageac run an hour on the river, and from the water you finally understand the valley: every castle sited to watch every bend.

Early one windless morning, with the village still asleep, I put the drone up over the river bend at Castelnaud (do check the current French rules — I've written a full guide to flying legally in Europe). The footage from that single flight justified carrying the extra kilogram for the whole trip.

## Practical notes for the solo traveler

Eating alone in France is a solved problem: eat your main meal at midday. The *menu du jour* is cheaper, the dining rooms are full of locals doing exactly the same thing, and a table for one at lunch raises no eyebrows anywhere. Evenings, I was happy with market cheese, bread, and a river bench.

I stayed in one place the entire time rather than hotel-hopping — a guesthouse just outside Sarlat's old town with parking, which matters more than charm when you're managing your own bags. Unpacking once is the older traveler's secret weapon: it turns a road trip into a residency.

Go in late May, June, or September. The light is long, the temperatures are kind to walkers, and the valley is yours before ten and after five. The Dordogne does not ask you to be young. It asks you to pay attention — and attention, at this point in life, is the one thing we have in abundance.
